GARE MONTPARNASSE (THE MELANCHOLY OF DEPARTURE)

GIORGIO DE CHIRICO, 1914

Okay. We got a train station. A clock up on a brick tower. Stuck at twenty-seven past one. An empty square. A little train on the horizon, puffing smoke. Two tiny figures way off, just standing there. And down in the corner, a pile of bananas. Green ones. Not even ripe. Just sitting there like they know something. For about one second, it all looks normal. Then it tips. Look at the ground on the right, ramping up too steep, too fast. The arcade on the left pulls one way, the building behind it pulls another. The wind's whipping the flags on that tower. But the train's smoke goes straight up. Nothing lines up. That's the trick. The bananas are a decoy. The thing crawling up your back is the geometry. He built a place that can't exist, and painted it like it's the most normal thing in the world. And he called it The Melancholy of Departure. Like, you can't even tell if that train's coming or going, but you already miss it. He painted it in early 1914. Here's the kicker. De Chirico is twenty-five. Twenty-five, and he's already found a frequency nobody else can hear yet. Empty plazas. Long shadows. Ya know that feeling when nobody's following you, but you pick up the pace anyway. A few years later, the Surrealists find this. Dalí, Magritte, Ernst, all of them walk through this exact door. And by the time they move in, he's gone. He drops the whole thing, goes classical, starts painting like it's three hundred years ago. They turn on him. But, the painting already did its job. It built the room. They moved in later. He'd already moved out. A few years later, he finds out these early paintings are still the ones people want. So he makes more. Sometimes he gives the new ones old dates. This one really is from 1914. Some of the later ones? Same artist. But wrong year. There's an Italian word for them. Verifalsi. True fakes.
