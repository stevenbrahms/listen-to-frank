No. 5/No. 24

Mark Rothko, 1948 · Gallery 403 · Floor 4

Rothko said his pictures were dramas. And the shapes were the actors. He wanted them to be able to move around, make big gestures, without getting embarrassed. Kind of a lot to ask of a blob, but try it. Look at this like a stage. Up top, almost nothing. Just cream, with a thin orange-red line around the edge, like he’s painted the frame on. Then that wide khaki band across the middle. Dark and smoky on the left. And everybody’s crowded down below it. Find the black egg in the middle. It’s got orange around it, then gray, then a pale halo with some pink in it. Like there’s a spotlight on it. Down to the right, that bristly black thing. Farther right, a rusty little box with thick bars across it. Over on the left, shapes are running into each other. Something pointed, some smears, a pale cloud. And one black bar off by itself. Down in the corner, that tiny rust-colored squiggle. Almost didn’t get in. So maybe these are the auditions. And the big blocks of color he paints for the rest of his career? Those are the ones who got the part. That black egg gets its halo. The little squiggle gets a corner. You can start wondering who’s doing what to whom. Or just look at the shapes. You don’t have to give everybody a job. Around this time, he pretty much stops giving his paintings names. Mostly numbers. And the numbers keep starting over. So years later, when this museum gives Rothko his big show, there are two paintings on the list called Number Twenty-Four. This is one of them.
