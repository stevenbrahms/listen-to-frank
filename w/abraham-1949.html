Abraham

Barnett Newman, 1949

Nearly seven feet tall. Black on very dark brown. One black stripe running down it, just left of center. That’s pretty much it. You can see why somebody might think it’s unfinished. Or a prank. Barnett Newman paints this in 1949. A year earlier, he’d started making paintings with a vertical stripe running through a field of color. He keeps coming back to it. Different colors. Different sizes. Eventually, you see that stripe, you know who painted it. Before that, he’d destroyed most of his old paintings. Years of work. Gone. Which is one way to deal with your early stuff. Then he just started over. From scratch, he said. Like painting didn’t exist. And after the Second World War, starting over meant something bigger to him. Beauty. Harmony. Everything nicely arranged. He doesn’t think that stuff is enough anymore. So look at what he’s left himself. A dark field. A darker stripe. Even the stripe’s hard to separate from what’s around it. Your eye keeps trying to pull it forward. Now, the title. Abraham. His father’s name. His father had died two years earlier. And there’s also the other Abraham. You know, the one from the Bible. The one God tells to take his son up a mountain and sacrifice him. Quite a name to put on something that’s supposedly got nothing in it. Newman doesn’t paint the mountain. Or the father. Or the son. He gives you this. And the size matters. He compared looking at this painting to coming face to face with another man. So look again. Nearly seven feet tall. Narrow. Upright. Roughly the size of a grown man. No eyes. No mouth. Nothing you can read an expression from. You can’t tell what he’s thinking. But it feels like somebody’s there. And Newman gave it his father’s name.
