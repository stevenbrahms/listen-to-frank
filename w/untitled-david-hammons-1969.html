Untitled

David Hammons, 1969 · Gallery 420 · Floor 4

Alright, so he’s covered in grease. Lying on his own picture. And now he’s got to get up without wrecking it. That’s David Hammons. Young Black artist in Los Angeles, late sixties. So... he greases up his face, his hands, even his clothes, and presses himself against a board. Calls these body prints. It’s his first series. But then he’s got to get off the thing. Three minutes, he said. Maybe longer. Just lying there figuring out how to do that without smudging it. Once he’s up, out comes a kitchen strainer. He shakes powdered color over the mark. It catches where the grease is. And there he is. His head’s tipped way back, face to the sky. Barely a face. A couple of dark smudges, mouth and eye. His hands are together, up by his chin. Kinda looks like he’s praying. He said the body gives him the truth, whether he wants it or not. And he likes the surprise. Sometimes he can hardly believe what comes out. And people want these things. He’s selling some, giving others away, making more. He said later they were doing so well he had to get out. Money coming in hand over fist. And the woman who ran his gallery says he had a rule. He shouldn’t have to buy stuff to make art. These were the last things he made with art-store supplies. So he just stops. Goes off and makes work out of stuff he doesn’t have to buy. Hair swept off barbershop floors. Barbecue bones. Bottle caps. He ends up getting called one of the most important artists in America. And at one point he’s on a New York sidewalk selling snowballs. Lined up by size, different prices. Anyway, look at that hand again. The rest of him’s fading in and out. But that hand’s sharp. You’ve got the creases across his palm, the little ridges on his fingers. He didn’t sit there drawing those. They’re his.
