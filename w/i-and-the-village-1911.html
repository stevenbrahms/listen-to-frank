I and the Village

Marc Chagall, 1911 · Gallery 503 · Floor 5

Green face. Cow. Nose to nose. Both taking this very seriously. Look at their eyes. Chagall’s drawn a thin line between them, like they’re in the middle of something. Now look up into the pink. A guy with a scythe is walking along, and next to him a woman’s upside down, arms out. Some of the houses are upside down too. And inside the cow’s cheek, there’s a woman milking another cow. Nobody seems bothered. Marc Chagall. Grew up in a Jewish family in Vitebsk. A city, actually, more than half Jewish when he was a kid. His dad worked hauling herring. His grandfather was a butcher. And his uncle used to take him out in a bumpy cart to look for cattle. He loved those trips. So this cow isn’t some strange thing he dreamed up in Paris. He grew up around these animals. Practically a neighbor. He paints this the year after he gets to Paris. All around him, the Cubists are breaking things into pieces. Bottles, tables, faces. He likes what they’re doing. Look at those circles and diagonal lines cutting through the two heads. He’s trying it too. They break up bottles. He breaks up memories. And he puts them wherever he feels like. One night, the poet Apollinaire, the guy who’ll later make up the word surrealism, comes to his studio. Chagall talks, Apollinaire listens, then sits down and mutters something about the supernatural. Fair enough. Chagall will keep coming back to this place in his paintings for the rest of his life. Then, thirty years after painting this, he has to get out of France. He’s Jewish. This museum sends an invitation that helps him and his wife escape to New York. The painting’s already here, on loan. It stays through the war. Chagall arrives the day before Germany invades the Soviet Union. Within weeks, German troops reach Vitebsk. The Jews who are still there are forced into a ghetto. That October, the Germans take thousands of them outside the city and shoot them.
