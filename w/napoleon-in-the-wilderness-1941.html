Napoleon in the Wilderness

Max Ernst, 1941 · Gallery 522 · Floor 5

Find the guy on the left, in the big hat. Arms folded. One foot up on a rock. Keep him in mind. Max Ernst, the Surrealist, is in an old brick factory in the south of France. France is at war with Germany. Ernst is German. Spent four years in the German artillery the last time around. So they lock him up. In a brick oven. Brick dust in his food, he says. In the pores of his skin. A friend writes a letter. He gets out. Then they arrest him again. Leonora Carrington, the painter he lives with, watches them take him away. Eventually, the French release him. Now he’s trying to cross into Spain. According to Ernst, his visa’s expired. The stationmaster takes his passport. Then hands it back and walks him over to two trains. One goes to Spain. One goes back into France. “Be very careful not to take the wrong train.” Ernst takes the wrong train. Ten minutes later, he’s in Spain. Months later, he’s in California, finishing a painting he started in France. This one. The museum later calls it his first painting in America. Look at how he makes it. Wet paint. Press a flat sheet against it. Peel it away. Basically painting by accident. He turns the canvas upside down and does it again. Then looks through the mess for something he recognizes. Remember the guy in the hat? One eye. A snout. Napoleon, Ernst says. On St. Helena. In exile. He wrote that maybe it had to do with how he was feeling, without him knowing it. The tall woman in red is often read as Carrington. They didn’t get out together. Follow the horn she’s holding. Green creature on the end. Teeth. Looking straight at the guy in the hat. Now, about the passport. Before he gets it back, customs makes him open a package of paintings he’s kept aside. It’s full of these blotchy accidents. Thank heavens, he writes. A little exhibition starts up, right there in customs. And the stationmaster says: “Monsieur, I respect talent.”
