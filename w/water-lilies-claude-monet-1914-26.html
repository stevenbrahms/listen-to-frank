Water Lilies

Claude Monet, 1914-26 · Gallery 515 · Floor 5

One of Monet’s gardeners worked from a boat. Dust kept blowing in off the road and settling on the water, and Monet wanted clean reflections. So somebody had to literally dust the pond. He’d had it dug in the first place so he’d have something to paint. And here’s nearly forty-two feet of it, with no bank, no horizon. The only sky is the one in the water. Try those pale patches in the middle. They’re clouds. Follow one for a moment, and you’re looking down into something deep. Then you notice a leaf, and you’re back on the surface. Same stretch of paint, doing both. Get closer, and even the water disappears. You’ve just got thick paint, scrubbed paint, one color dragged through another. He builds a whole studio for these during the First World War. He writes a friend that some days he feels ashamed, painting his pond while all those people are dying. He keeps going until he dies. Then, for years, the paintings just sit in the studio. People think they’re too messy. During the next war, Giverny gets bombarded. Glass and metal fall onto the canvases and cut into them. Somebody patches the cuts with tape. Then, in the fifties, painters in New York are working huge. Pollock, with paint going right out to the edges. Suddenly, messy looks right. This museum, which had mostly left Monet out of its story of modern art, finally wants one. A curator, Dorothy Miller, and a colleague go pick one out at Monet’s studio. Alfred Barr wires back, in telegram-speak: BUY MONET IF YOU DOROTHY REALLY LIKE. They do. The museum buys it. Three years later, the building catches fire. A worker dies. The Monet’s too big to move, and it’s destroyed. Afterward, Dorothy goes looking for another. She finds this one in another painter’s studio in Paris, tape still on the cuts. Back in New York, conservators vacuum thirty years of dust off the back. Then they put the cut threads back together, thread to thread. Nearly forty-two feet of pond. Go on. See if you can find a repair.
