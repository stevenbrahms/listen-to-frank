CUBIC-MODULAR WALL STRUCTURE, BLACK

SOL LEWITT, 1966

Go ahead, count the openings. Five across. Five down. Twenty-five little squares with nothing in them. It's wood, painted black, and it hangs where a painting would hang. Except a painting usually has a picture. This has holes. The title is Cubic-Modular Wall Structure, Black. Nobody is overselling anything. Step off to one side. It sticks out about nine inches, and every strip throws a thin shadow. Most of what you're looking at is the white wall behind it. The museum is doing half the work. Now here's the part LeWitt liked. The cube is boring. That's not an insult. That was his pitch. In 1966, the year he made this, he wrote that the cube was "relatively uninteresting." No drama. No motion. Barely a feeling in it. Perfect. Most artists spend years trying to put themselves into the work. LeWitt was trying to get himself out. Pick one plain shape. Set a rule. Repeat it. No beautiful brushstroke. No big emotional decision. Just the same empty cube, over and over. Except they never quite stay the same. Move a little, and the shadows change. The empty parts turn solid. The solid parts disappear. The most boring shape he could find won't sit still. LeWitt used to work at this museum. The book counter first. Then the night desk. Dan Flavin worked here. Robert Ryman. Robert Mangold. Basically a future gallery roster punching a clock. Alicia Legg worked here too. She knew LeWitt from those days and watched him start showing downtown. When MoMA decided to give him a retrospective in 1978, they handed it to her. Her explanation was pretty simple. "I guess because they knew I knew him." Years later, she gave the museum this. Twenty-five empty squares. The night-desk guy, on the wall.
