Pastry Case, I

Claes Oldenburg, 1961–62 · Gallery 408 · Floor 4

Claes Oldenburg bought the case. The kind they sell at restaurant supply places on the Bowery. Bought the plates, too. And the little metal sundae cups. He made the food. Burlap soaked in plaster. Hardware-store enamel, straight from the can. There's a banana split up top. A wedge of blue pie. Down below, something long and brown on a blue plate. Looks like ribs. In a pastry case. In 1961, years before he's famous for making everyday things giant, Oldenburg rents an empty storefront on East Second Street. Sixty bucks a month. Steam heat included. In the back, he makes the merchandise. Pastries. Shirts. Girdles. In the front, he sells it. By December, he's open. He's got posters. A business name. Ray Gun Manufacturing Company. Friday, Saturday, Sunday. One to six. And price tags with those little ninety-five-cent endings, like the other stores. Strawberry shortcake, eighty-four ninety-five. A cookie, twenty-nine ninety-five. Comes with the plate. So you can walk into an actual shop, pick something out, pay for it, and take it home. You just can't eat the cake. Or wear the shirt. The neighbors mostly stay out. Pretty terrified, Oldenburg said. They didn't know what was going on. Andy Warhol comes in and buys a shirt. The shop stays open for two months. When it closes, he's sold over sixteen hundred dollars' worth. The gallery takes its cut. Before he ever opened the doors, he'd written a list of what he wanted art to do. This was on it. "I am for an art that is political-erotical-mystical, that does something other than sit on its ass in a museum."
