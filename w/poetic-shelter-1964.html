POETIC SHELTER

LYGIA CLARK, 1964

Okay, this thing is hinged. Black metal plates attached to each other, and the whole thing folds. About two feet across. Five inches tall. Big rounded shapes, sorta like lily pads, except one comes to a point. And look at the top. Somebody folded that piece up and suddenly it has an inside. Clark called these things Bichos. Portuguese for critters. Which makes sense. A critter doesn't sit still. So, you're supposed to pick one up, fold it, and get a different sculpture. Fold it again. Different again. She made around seventy of them. Some geometric, some curved, some harder to describe. And they got names like The Crab. The Machine. This one is Poetic Shelter. One she called Bird in Space. There's another Bird in Space nearby. Brancusi's. He spent years getting that one shape exactly right. Clark's doesn't have one shape. You fold it, that's the sculpture. Fold it again, now that's the sculpture. This one needs you. Which gets a little awkward in here. Because you're in a museum. No touching. Clark wanted you to pick it up. So what you're seeing is just one position. And obviously they're not going to let you grab a Lygia Clark and start folding. That's the problem. And she keeps going with this. If what matters is what happens between the thing and your body... eventually, do you even need the thing? By the seventies she's working one-on-one with people in her apartment in Rio. Stones. Shells. Bags of air. Things placed on the body. She called the people who came to her clients. When MoMA gave her a retrospective in 2014, they hit the same wall. The real Bichos were there. You couldn't touch them. So they put out copies you could. Which is kind of funny. The copies got to move. But the real ones had to sit still.
