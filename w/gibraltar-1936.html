Gibraltar

Alexander Calder, 1936 · Gallery 522 · Floor 5

So, a wood so heavy it sinks. It’s called lignum vitae. Drop a block in a bucket and it goes straight to the bottom. That’s the lump underneath this thing. Split, pitted, looking like nobody’s touched it. Except for that dark surface down low. Somebody’s been at that. Then a piece of polished walnut cuts across it on a slant, smooth as a spoon. There’s a white ball at the low end, a red one up on a thin rod, and way up top, a little wooden crescent. Almost like a rock with a bit of sky over it. This is Calder. The mobile guy. The man who got sculpture moving, working here with a wood that won’t even float. The year he makes this, he has a show in New York. There’s a pencil sheet listing the works, each one a little doodle with a number and a price. This one’s number twenty-one. A scribbled peak with that slanted plate across it. Two hundred. No name yet. That same year, a dealer puts Calder in a book on Surrealism. But he can’t quite place him. Sometimes Surrealist, sometimes abstract. He wants Calder to pick a side. Calder’s got rough wood and polished walnut in the same piece. Look where they meet. The rough stuff comes up from underneath and pokes out above that smooth plate. A sculptor friend had already called the ones that don’t move “stabiles.” It’s a dig, but Calder kept the word. This is one. He later says names are tags. If he had to talk about a piece, he’d just make a little drawing. Number twenty-one does get a tag eventually. Gibraltar. As in the rock. This museum’s own catalogue lists it: Gibraltar. Stabile.
