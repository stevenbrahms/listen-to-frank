WALL DRAWING #1144, BROKEN BANDS OF COLOR IN FOUR DIRECTIONS

SOL LEWITT, 2004

This whole wall can disappear. Painted over. Gone. And MoMA would still own the artwork. That's the trick. Thirty-seven feet of color. Broken bands running off in four directions. Bright. Orderly. Almost stupidly cheerful. But the permanent part isn't on the wall. It's a set of instructions. Somebody gave them to MoMA anonymously in 2004. The first one went up in October 1968. A new gallery on Prince Street. A benefit to help end the war in Vietnam. LeWitt walked up to a bare wall with a pencil and drew straight onto it. The price list didn't give it a normal price. It just said: by the hour. You weren't buying that wall. You were buying his time. That's still how it works. LeWitt made the decisions. Other people made this version. Band by band. Color by color. Following his instructions. Like a score. The composer writes it. Somebody else plays it. And for a guy who put the idea first, he let the result have some fun. Not some grey little puzzle daring you to understand it. Look at the thing. The instructions go in. Something like stained glass comes out. When this wall comes down, they'll paint over it. No funeral. The instructions can make it again somewhere else. Different wall. Different hands. Same work. LeWitt died in 2007. The next year, more than sixty artists and students spent six months covering nearly an acre of walls in Massachusetts with a hundred and five of his drawings. He wasn't there.
