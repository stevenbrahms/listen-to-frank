Girl Before a Mirror

Pablo Picasso, 1932 · Gallery 517 · Floor 5

So Marie-Thérèse Walter is seventeen, out shopping for a collar, when a random guy walks up and takes her by the arm. Says she has an interesting face. That they’re going to do great things together. And then he says, “I’m Picasso.” This means nothing to her. She just notices his tie. Red and black. So he shows her a book about himself, in Chinese or Japanese. He thought that was funny. You couldn’t read his name on it. He’s forty-five. Married, with a child at home. For years, he keeps her secret. Even works her initials, M and T, into paintings of guitars, where his wife won’t notice them. Five years later, he’s fifty, with a chauffeur, a château, and a huge Paris retrospective coming up. He thinks this show will decide whether he still matters. All winter and spring, he paints Marie-Thérèse. He said this was the one he liked best. Didn’t say why. Look at her face. That lilac profile on one side, and the hot yellow face looking straight out on the other. Then follow her arm across the mirror’s post. The woman in the reflection looks darker. Heavier. Picasso never explained her. Other people have. Maybe the girl’s older self. Her shadow. Paintings of a girl at a mirror used to carry a little warning. The face doesn’t last. Anyway, that June, the retrospective opens. Portraits of his wife and son get the place of honor. But Marie-Thérèse’s face is all through the recent paintings. Picasso doesn’t go to the opening. He goes to the movies instead. Later, MoMA’s director chooses this for the museum. Calls it the finest Picasso in ten years. Decades later, they X-ray it. Turns out under those stripes down her side, she started out with her back to you. Picasso painted over her fast. Black lines and house paint.
