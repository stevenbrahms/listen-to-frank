Migrant Mother

Dorothea Lange, 1936

Her name was Florence Owens Thompson. For more than forty years, almost nobody looking at this knew that. California, March, 1936. Dorothea Lange's been on the road a month, photographing the Depression for the government. She's tired. Headed home. Drives right past a sign. Pea-pickers' camp. Another twenty miles before she gives up arguing with herself, turns around, and drives back. The pea crop's frozen. Families are stranded there. Nothing to pick. Nothing coming in. She approaches a tent. A woman, thirty-two. Kids folded around her. Lange takes a few photographs, moving closer. Never asks her name. Now look at the one everybody remembers. The hand at the jaw. Two kids turned into her shoulders. A baby asleep in her lap. Three children leaning on one person. She's looking somewhere past all of them. Lange brings her photographs and news of the camp to the San Francisco News. The paper covers the emergency. The government sends twenty thousand pounds of food. But Thompson and her family have already moved on. They get none of it. The photograph keeps going. Newspapers. Magazines. Eventually, museum walls. Migrant Mother. The face of the Depression. But her family later tells a story you can't see in the picture. They'd been driving to other farm work when their car broke down. Her partner and two of her boys had hauled the radiator into town to get it fixed. She was waiting for them to come back. And she was Cherokee. Born in Indian Territory, before it became Oklahoma. Forty-two years after the photograph, a reporter finds her. She says she wishes it had never been taken. Feels she's been used. And she remembers something Lange told her that day. "She said she'd send me a copy." "She never did."
