NO. 5/NO. 22

MARK ROTHKO, 1950 (dated on reverse 1949)

By 1950, the rectangles have pretty much locked in. But this one still has the scaffolding up. Get close to the red band. Three thin white lines run through it, wobbling like a pulse. Within a few years, little details like that are gone. The surfaces get quieter. More sealed off. Right here, he’s still figuring it out. Now say something nice about the colors. Go ahead. It would have made him furious. In 1956, a writer named Selden Rodman got Rothko talking for a book of interviews. Rothko told him flat out that he was not an abstractionist. He wasn’t interested in color for its own sake. He was interested in basic human emotions. Tragedy. Ecstasy. Doom. The colors weren’t the subject. They were the cast. Rothko read Nietzsche. He thought in tragedies. To him, these weren’t nice arrangements of red and yellow. They were things acting on each other. Watch the red leaning into the yellow. That’s the drama. So don’t stand here matching the orange to your couch. That is exactly the kind of compliment he couldn’t stand. Then Rothko told Rodman something even stranger. People broke down and cried in front of these paintings. And he believed they were having the same religious experience he had while painting them. Not “moved.” Not “touched.” Crying. Rothko was completely sure these things worked. He’d watched it happen. But ask him how colored mud on a piece of cloth reaches into a stranger and turns something over, and he couldn’t tell you. He knew it happened. That was enough. So stop naming the colors for a second and see whether anything happens to you.
