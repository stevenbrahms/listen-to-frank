F-111

JAMES ROSENQUIST, 1964-65

America doesn't introduce itself slowly. It throws ten things at your head. Half of them delicious. One of them fatal. James Rosenquist made a painting that works the same way. Eighty-six feet long. Ten feet tall. Built to take over a room. Rosenquist painted billboards. Minneapolis first, then New York, up on the scaffolding over Times Square in his mid-twenties. Whiskey ads. Movie faces two stories tall. Then he quit, went downtown, and aimed the whole trick back at the country that trained him. Look what the billboard painter put up. A little girl smiling under a salon hair dryer that fits her like a warhead. An angel food cake. A Firestone tire. A fork in spaghetti, big as plumbing. And running underneath all of it, nose to tail, the F-111. The newest fighter-bomber America was building, while Vietnam climbed up the news. Near the end, a beach umbrella. On a fucking mushroom cloud. The plane and the cake get the same treatment. Same gloss. Same loving finish. A sales pitch and a threat display on one surface. Your eye can't find the seam. It's beautiful. It has to be. That's how the poison gets in. April 1965. All four walls at Leo Castelli. People stood inside it, surrounded, the way you're surrounded now. And the panels were meant to be sold off one at a time. A slice of cake here. A slab of bomber over somebody's couch. Rosenquist called that a dark joke. Buy a panel, and you own a souvenir of a weapons system your taxes already paid for. But on the last day of the show, one buyer took the whole thing. Reportedly, sixty thousand dollars. Robert Scull, who made his money in taxis. Twenty-one years later, Sotheby's sold it out of his estate. Two point one million. The souvenir did fine.
