F-111

JAMES ROSENQUIST, 1964-65

Okay. There's no good place to start. There's a little girl under a salon hair dryer. Then angel food cake. A Firestone tire. A fork lifting spaghetti. And running underneath all of it, nose to tail, the F-111. Eighty-six feet long. Ten feet tall. Wrapped around four walls. So you don't really look at this painting. You basically stand inside the sales pitch. Rosenquist used to paint billboards over Times Square. Whiskey. Movies. Faces two stories tall. You know, every image huge, clean, and trying to sell you something. Then he takes that trick and puts it on a fighter-bomber. The newest plane America was building while the war in Vietnam kept getting worse. And Rosenquist gives it the full treatment. Bright. Shiny. Brand new. Then runs it through everything else America was selling. Cake. Tires. Hair dryers. Spaghetti. And near the end, a beach umbrella. On a fucking mushroom cloud. Somehow, all of it belongs together. The bomber and the cake get equal care. Glossed up. Different products. Same smile. And your eye just keeps moving because every part looks good. It takes a second to notice what it's moving across. Now, when the painting opened at Leo Castelli in 1965, people stood inside it. Surrounded, the way you're surrounded now. And the panels were supposed to be sold separately. A slice of cake here. A slab of bomber there. You know. Something for every room. Rosenquist called that a dark joke. Buy a panel, and you own a souvenir of a weapons system your taxes already paid for. Retail twice. Then one couple took the whole thing. Paid for it with taxi money. Literally. They owned a fleet of cabs. Probably for the best. This thing does not want to behave over a couch. It wants the room.
