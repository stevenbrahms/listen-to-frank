The Piano Lesson

Henri Matisse, 1916 · Gallery 506 · Floor 5

This one, you need the year. 1916. France is deep in the war. Matisse has tried to enlist. Bought the boots and everything. They turn him down. Twice. Too old. Weak heart. His mother’s alone in the occupied north. His brother’s in a prison camp. He’s sold prints to send them bread. A hundred kilos a week. And his two boys are getting close to military age. Here’s one of them. Pierre. Sixteen. At home, practicing the piano. The painting’s eight feet tall. And not one thing in it about the war. Find his head, over on the right. Just above the music. One eye looking out at you. The other disappears under that gray triangle. So, who’s giving the lesson? That person sitting up there, all upright with no face. Looks like somebody’s keeping an eye on him. But that’s another painting. Hanging on the living room wall. Matisse has flattened the room. Cut it into hard edges and angles. He’s borrowing from Cubism. Picasso’s territory. Picasso said nobody looked at his paintings as closely as Matisse. And nobody looked at Matisse’s as closely as Picasso. And here he is, using it on his own son. Now find the metronome. On the piano. There’s your teacher. Another triangle. Like the one cutting into Pierre’s face. And with everything else so still, you can almost hear it. Tick. Tick. Tick. The next summer, Matisse’s older boy signs up. He’s going to work on airplanes. Matisse writes to a painter friend. “Something important has happened in my house.” “Jean, my eldest, has left to join the regiment at Dijon.”
