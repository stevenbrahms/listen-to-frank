PINES AND ROCKS (FONTAINEBLEAU?)

PAUL CÉZANNE, 1897

There's a question mark in the title of this one. Cézanne. Pines and Rocks. Then, in parentheses, Fontainebleau. Question mark. So what's up with the question mark? It's a forest near Paris. But for years, people thought this was Provence. Cézanne's home turf. Half of France away. Look at it. Rusty red pines. Grey rocks going pink. Blue between the branches. Gorgeous. But good luck getting an address out of it. Now look for the pale gaps between the brushstrokes. That's bare canvas. And where there is paint, it's thin. Almost watercolor. For years, scholars dated this to 1904. Provence, they figured. But the thin paint didn't fit. His final paintings are thick, slanting strokes. That pushed the date back to around 1897. He was near Paris then, so the location moved too. Possibly that forest. Hence the question mark. Anyway, back to those bare patches. Cézanne was fifty-six before anybody gave him his own show. 1895, a dealer's shop in Paris. Pissarro went, and wrote his son about it. The still lifes, perfect. The unfinished ones, he said, even better. Degas bought some. By the end of his life, younger painters were looking to Cézanne for answers. Matisse bought one of his paintings, kept it thirty-seven years. Said he drew his faith and perseverance from it. Another painter, Émile Bernard, went to meet him. They exchanged letters. A year before he died, Cézanne wrote to Bernard about something he was struggling with. Trying to paint what he saw. The colors, the light, where one thing meets another. He couldn't get it all onto the canvas. The painting stayed incomplete. Now look back at those bare patches. He'd painted this years before, already leaving gaps he'd later struggle to explain.
