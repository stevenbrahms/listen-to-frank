Three Musicians

Pablo Picasso, 1921

Look at these guys. Three of them, crammed together, making music. A clarinet. A guitar. A monk holding the sheet music. And there’s a dog. His head’s over on the left. Follow him under the table, all the way to that tail sticking up between the guitar player’s legs. He’s taking up quite a bit of the room, actually. Now, the three people. They’ve often been read as Picasso and two of his closest friends. The harlequin in the middle, wearing the diamonds? Picasso used that character for himself over and over. Here, he gets the guitar. The one in white, on the clarinet, could be the poet Guillaume Apollinaire. And the monk could be another poet, Max Jacob. But this is 1921. Apollinaire is already dead. He died two days before the First World War ended. Jacob’s still around. But that year, he’s left Paris for a town on the Loire, to live near an abbey church. Writing. Praying. Pretty close to the costume Picasso’s given him. So, one friend’s dead. The other’s gone. And if that’s who these three are, look where Picasso’s put them. Back in one room. All dressed up. Band going. Look how bright their suits are. Then look at the room. Dark brown. Bare walls. Hardly enough space for everybody. Especially with that dog. Now try following one body all the way around. Find where he ends and the next guy starts. Good luck. You’re following a patch of color, thinking it belongs to somebody’s body. Then it seems to be part of the table. Then you’ve lost it in the wall. It all looks like cut paper. Flat pieces, fitted together. And Picasso had been doing that with actual paper for years. Back around 1912, working through Cubism, he starts gluing things into his pictures. Newspaper. Wallpaper. Sheet music. So look at the monk’s music again. You could easily think that’s a piece of paper he’s stuck on. Back then, it might have been. Here, he’s painted it. All those pieces, and it’s all paint.
