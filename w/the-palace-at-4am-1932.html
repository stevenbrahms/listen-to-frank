The Palace at 4 a.m.

Alberto Giacometti, 1932 · Gallery 517 · Floor 5

It fits on a table. Which is almost funny, because look at the title. The Palace at 4 A.M. Sounds huge. Marble, servants, somebody crying on a balcony. And it's this. A rickety little frame of wood and wire and string. About two feet tall. More like a stage set nobody finished. This is Giacometti years before the skinny walking man he's famous for. He's around thirty, running with the Surrealists, and he falls hard for a woman. Six months, completely gone. And the way he tells it, they build a palace together at night. Out of matchsticks. Somebody moves wrong and a whole section falls down. So they start again. Build it. Watch it collapse. Build it again. Then the relationship ends. A year later, this thing starts forming in his head. Little by little. Every piece finding its place. By autumn, he says, it's so clear that building it takes just one day. Now, he couldn't explain everything in here. But he gave us a few clues. Wrote them up for a Surrealist magazine. That stiff little figure on the left? His mother, as he remembered her from childhood. Long black dress, down to the floor. And that long wooden shape in the middle, leaning against the white board? He said he identified with it. So you've got his mother over there. Him in the middle. And the woman? Look at the spine hanging inside that cage on the right. He said she sold him a spine on one of the very first nights they met. He connects that spine, and the bird skeleton above it, with the beginning and end of their relationship. Doesn't give you a neat explanation. Just puts them in the same little palace. His mother. Him. Those bones. Next summer he tells André Breton, the guy in charge of the Surrealists, he can't make a thing that doesn't have something to do with her. The relationship didn't last. This did. And it still looks like one wrong move would bring it down.
