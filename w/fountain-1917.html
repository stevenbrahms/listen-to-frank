Fountain

Marcel Duchamp, 1917

So, this is the urinal they wouldn't let into a show that was supposed to let everybody in. New York. Thousands of artworks. No jury. No prizes. Pay the fee, you're in. That was the deal. Now the directors are in a meeting, trying to work out what to do about a urinal. Somebody's signed it R. Mutt. Some say it's indecent. Others say, well, we did say everybody. They take a vote. Out. One of the directors resigns. Marcel Duchamp. Keep that name in mind. Now look at the thing. A store-bought urinal. Turned on its back. Little holes where the water comes through. And there on the rim, in black paint: R. Mutt. A fake name. The usual story is, Duchamp sent it in himself. Duchamp and some friends put out a magazine. The editorial takes up Mr. Mutt's case. Whether he made it himself "has no importance." "He chose it." Duchamp calls these readymades. He said he went for the thing with the least chance of being liked. Years later, there's another vote. Five hundred art experts. Most influential modern artwork. The urinal wins. Picasso comes second. But there's something else about Mr. Mutt. The day after the show opens, Duchamp writes to his sister. Says a female friend sent it in under a man's name. Doesn't say which friend. Some people think he means Baroness Elsa, another Dada artist. That argument's still going. And the original? Disappeared. The ones in museums are copies Duchamp approved decades later. The best look we've got at the first one is a photograph. Alfred Stieglitz took it. Put it on a pedestal in his gallery. In front of a painting. Made a picture. Two weeks after they throw it out, Stieglitz writes to a friend. Everybody who's seen the photograph, he says, thinks it's beautiful. "A cross between a Buddha and a veiled woman."
