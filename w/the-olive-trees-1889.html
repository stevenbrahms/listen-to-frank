The Olive Trees

Vincent van Gogh, 1889 · Gallery 501 · Floor 5

Alright, so… look at the mountains first. That dark blue ridge, left of the middle. Two holes in it. Like somebody punched straight through the rock. Keep those in mind. It’s the summer of 1889. Van Gogh’s living at the asylum in the south of France. When he’s well enough, he goes out to paint the olive trees. That spring, he writes his brother: “if you could only see them.” Says the sound of an olive grove is “very intimate, immensely old.” But he’s having trouble painting them. The leaves keep changing color on him. Silver, sometimes blue, sometimes greenish. Look how the curves in the ground run up through the trunks and into the leaves. He’s painting The Starry Night around this time, too. Those big swirls in the sky. Here, he’s got that same rhythm running through the dirt. Anyway, that autumn, two painter friends send him pictures of their new stuff. Paul Gauguin and Émile Bernard. They’ve both painted Christ in the Garden of Olives. Jesus among the trees. Gauguin gives his Christ bright red hair. Then Vincent writes back to Bernard. Calls his painting a “nightmare.” To his brother, it’s even worse. In those Christs, he says, “nothing is observed.” Bernard has “probably never seen an olive tree.” But if you keep reading that letter to Bernard, he says he liked his earlier paintings. People walking in a field. Trees against a blue sky. He wants that painter back. “Become a little more yourself again,” he says. He tells Bernard you can paint anxiety, or give somebody comfort, through the things right around you. Then he describes the asylum garden. The big pine trees. The earth burnt by the sun. Anyway, that November, Vincent’s back out in the real groves. Morning and evening, through those bright, cold days. Five more canvases. About fifteen olive paintings by the end of the year. This one’s from the start of it. OK, so go back to those holes. People have looked at them and read his illness into them. Figured he must have invented something that strange. But it turns out that’s a real rock outside Saint-Rémy. Les Deux Trous. The Two Holes. You can walk up there and see them.
