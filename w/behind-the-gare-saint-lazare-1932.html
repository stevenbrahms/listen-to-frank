Behind the Gare Saint-Lazare

Henri Cartier-Bresson, 1932

Alright, look at his heel. That little gap between the shoe and the water. He hasn't landed yet. Paris, 1932. Behind Saint-Lazare Station. Cartier-Bresson's on the other side of a wooden fence. There are repairs going on. A huge puddle. And a gap between the boards. He jams his little Leica up to it. Clicks. And gets this. A man in midair. His whole reflection jumping underneath him. Now look behind him, at the poster. A dancer. Leg kicked out. Almost the same pose. The man, his reflection, the dancer. All jumping together. And photographers have been stalking puddles ever since. This becomes one of the famous examples of what gets called the decisive moment. The idea is that there's a fraction of a second when what's happening and the shapes it makes come together. The picture's there. Then it's gone. Twenty years later, Cartier-Bresson, by now one of the founders of the Magnum photo agency, publishes a book. In France, it's Images à la sauvette. Pictures grabbed on the run. His American publisher takes a phrase from an old quotation he's used inside and puts it on the cover. The Decisive Moment. Now his whole career has a motto. But go back to that fence. The gap wasn't even wide enough for the lens. A piece of board blocked the left side of the picture. He cropped it out. So this incredibly precise photograph comes through a hole that doesn't quite fit the camera. And you've got as long as you like to notice everything. The dancer. The reflection. That little gap under the shoe. He had a fraction of a second. Now look at the heel again. He's going to get wet. He's been about to get wet since 1932.
