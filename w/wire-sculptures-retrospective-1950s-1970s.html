Wire Sculptures

Ruth Asawa, 1950s–1970s

So Ruth Asawa learns how to make a simple egg basket, and turns that into these. Check out the long hanging ones. They swell out, pull back in, swell out again. Almost like they’re breathing. And then you notice there’s another whole shape inside some of them. She’s a Japanese American farm kid from outside Los Angeles. When she’s sixteen, the government puts her family in two horse stalls at a racetrack. Art school comes later. Then she spends a summer in Mexico, where someone shows her how to loop wire. One loop catches the next. You keep going, and you’ve got a surface. Look at the bowl down below. Still pretty much a basket. For the hanging ones, she keeps feeding the wire through until she can’t go any further. Nothing’s welded. A lobe, a waist, then another lobe. Now find a ball inside one of those lobes. In some of these, she’s turned that same mesh inward and just kept going. Critics were used to steel and stone sitting on a base. This hangs. She thought of it as drawing in space. She works on the floor at home in San Francisco. Imogen Cunningham photographs her there, pulling wire off a spool. And under the sculpture, there’s a baby with a bottle. Time magazine reviews her first New York show and calls her a San Francisco housewife. Years later, she says people can call it craft or art. Doesn’t bother her. Those are labels people put on things. Now look down at the platform under the sculpture. The shadow’s making another drawing. No wire.
