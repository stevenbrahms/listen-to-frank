Untitled

Kazuo Shiraga, 1964

There's a rope hanging from a studio ceiling in Japan. A man grabs it, swings out over a canvas on the floor, and paints with his feet. That's not a warm-up story. That's the method. Kazuo Shiraga worked this way for most of his career. He'd given up brushes. Then his hands. He said they knew too much. The paint sits so thick it casts shadows. Ridges lifting off the canvas. And that big sweeping arc on the right isn't a brushstroke. It's the path of a body. You can read the swing in it. Shiraga would lower himself into the paint and drag his full weight through it. And it wasn't flailing. As a teenager he studied judo and Western painting at once. He practiced by sketching plaster figures caught mid-action. Balance. Weight. Direction. He ran with Gutai. Postwar Japanese artists determined to make something nobody had made before. They didn't want to paint pictures of energy. They wanted energy to make the painting. One artist burst through paper screens. Another sent a remote-control toy car dripping paint across a surface. Shiraga wrestled a pile of mud. Then he found the rope and stayed with it for decades. The rope gives him the swing. It doesn't give him the ridges, the splashes, the skids. The paint pushes back. His feet slip. And after all that, look what came out. It's gorgeous. Almost entirely red, but never one red. Crimson sliding into burgundy, with something like fire underneath. The whole mass barely stays inside the edges. Raw canvas at the corners, like the painting was bigger than what it landed on. Then, bottom left, tiny black characters. His signature, sitting still beside all that churn. Seven years after this one, he took vows at a monastery on Mount Hiei. He never stopped painting. He just signed a different name. Sodō. It means Simple Path.
