Warm Broad Glow

Glenn Ligon, 2005

Two words in neon. negro sunshine. Sixteen feet across. All lowercase. And the letters are black. But you can read every word. This is Glenn Ligon's first neon. He gets the words from Gertrude Stein's Three Lives, published in 1909. She's describing a Black woman named Rose. Says Rose doesn't have the "wide, abandoned laughter" that makes "the warm broad glow of negro sunshine." Like there's a laugh she's supposed to have. Years later, a young Black writer named Richard Wright finds that book in a Chicago library. Native Son is years away. Right now, he's reading a white woman and hearing his grandmother. Then he reads a left-wing critic tearing into Stein in the paper. Calls her decadent. So Wright tries something. As he later tells it, he gathers Black stockyard workers in a basement and reads them the story these two words come from. They understand every word. Slapping their thighs. Interrupting him to say where they'd met a girl like that. They recognize people. Now, a lot of Ligon's work starts with somebody else's sentences. He stencils them in black. Layering them over and over. The words get harder to read toward the bottom. And when he gets to Stein, he's clear about the stereotypes. Says she's not giving you a full picture of Black humanity. But "negro sunshine" gives him something else to work with. Black joy. In a country he describes as mostly anti-Black, he says any expression of that joy is a kind of resistance. So he takes the words out of her sentence. Warm Broad Glow becomes the title. negro sunshine goes on the wall. He leaves the cords hanging down. Transformer boxes on the floor. And the fronts of the tubes are coated in black rubber. So the light comes out behind the words. A halo around a blackout. Every letter black. Still lighting up the wall.
