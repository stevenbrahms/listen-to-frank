Willows

Arthur Dove, 1940 · Gallery 509 · Floor 5

Arthur Dove lived on a boat for nine years. Which sounds nice until you get to the money. He’s broke. He and Helen Torr sail a forty-two-foot boat into Huntington Harbor, out on Long Island, and make it home. She’s a painter too. Everybody calls her Reds. Dove paints what’s around them. Trees, wind, water, the way the light changes. He’s often called the first American to paint a picture of nothing you could name. Though he doesn’t much like the word “abstraction.” There’s no such thing, he says. It’s extraction. And minding your own business. When this museum gets this painting, the press release calls it “abstract or near-abstract.” Keeping its options open. After the boat, Dove spends a few years upstate. The last year, he gets the biggest studio he’s ever had. A whole floor that used to be a roller-skating rink. Then it’s back to the salt water, into a one-room cottage beside a pond. Five hundred square feet. The place used to be a post office and general store. He has a heart attack. There’s kidney disease too, and some days he can’t get out of the house. Reds mostly gives up her own painting to look after him. Now look at this. All those green and gold curves bending over and down. The whole picture seems to droop, like a tree slowly closing around something. The edges are soft and feathery, until you get to that pale shape in the middle. It’s got windows. Two narrow ones, then a window divided into four. One pane’s yellow, as if somebody’s left a light on. There’s a door down at the bottom. Is that their cottage? He doesn’t say. But every spring, he makes a note in his diary. The day the willow outside his window puts out its first leaves.
