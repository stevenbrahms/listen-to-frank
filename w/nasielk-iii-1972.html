Nasielk III

Frank Stella, 1972 · Gallery 415 · Floor 4

So, Frank Stella’s on the radio. “What you see is what you see,” he says. The interviewer points out that doesn’t leave much. “I don’t know what else there is.” By then, he’s famous for black stripes. Painted with a house painter’s brush. This museum shows four of those paintings when he’s twenty-three. Black stripes. That’s it. Now look at this. Nine feet of cardboard and felt. Follow that maroon piece down the right. It turns the corner and runs along the bottom. A shelf. Three slats lean into it from the green side. And up top, that dark triangle tips away from the wall. There’s something behind a Stella now. Have a look under the shelf. Red. Orange. A little pink. A couple of years before making this, he’s in a hospital bed after knee surgery. His friend Richard Meier, the architect, brings him a book. Wooden Synagogues. Buildings in little Polish towns. Roofs piled on roofs. Most were destroyed in the war. Stella makes forty-two drawings in that hospital bed. They become a whole series. He says it’s about a culture being wiped out. Each work named for a town in the book. This one’s Nasielsk, north of Warsaw. Though the title’s missing an s. And Nasielsk’s synagogue was already gone before the war. It was falling apart in the eighteen hundreds. They took it down. Built a brick one instead. Almost sixty years before the Nazis arrived. Now go back to that triangle. Could be a roof. Those slats could be rafters. The shelf, a windowsill. Nothing’s changed while you’ve been standing here. Same cardboard. Same felt. “What you see is what you see.”
