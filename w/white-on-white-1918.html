White on White

Kazimir Malevich, 1918

"Swim in the white free abyss, infinity is before you." That's Kazimir Malevich, about his white paintings. This is one of them. A white square on a white square. That's a lot to ask... of a square. So, look closer. The background's warm, almost cream. The square's cool, almost gray. Tipped a little, like it slid. Neither one's really white. In 1915, he paints a black square on a white background. He calls this new approach Suprematism. Basically feeling gets top billing now. A painting doesn't have to show anything anymore. Shapes and colors alone can make you feel something. Then it's 1918. A year after the Russian Revolution. And Malevich goes further. Now even the black is gone. That's the white free abyss he meant. At first, the new Russia is into it. The state even buys his stuff. Then it isn't. Now it's 1927. Malevich takes about seventy works to a big show in Berlin. His one trip abroad. A month into the show, he heads home, figuring he's in trouble. He leaves the paintings with an architect friend in Berlin. He never sees them again. Three years later, back in Leningrad, the man who painted nothing gets arrested. Questioned. Let go. He goes back to painting peasants. And this one? Still in Germany. The architect passes them to a museum man in Hannover. Then the Nazis come in, and the museum man puts them in storage. So this white square, this "free abyss," it's sitting in the dark. Until 1935. This guy Alfred Barr, who runs the new Museum of Modern Art in New York, is over in Germany hunting for pictures. He finds the museum man. Buys two paintings. This is one of them. Buying them is one thing. Now he has to get them over the border. A suitcase and a couple of canvases rolled up inside his umbrella. Was this one in the umbrella? Maybe. Nobody knows which ones. But the painting gets out. That same year, Malevich dies in Leningrad. His friends put his coffin on an open truck and drive it down the main street. And on the hood of the truck... a black square.
