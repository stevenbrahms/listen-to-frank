WOMAN I

WILLEM DE KOONING, 1950–1952

Look at that smile. Look at those teeth. While he was working on this, de Kooning would cut mouths out of magazines and stick them on the painting. Advertising smiles. He said it helped to have something real there. Because the rest of her's getting pretty far from, you know, looking like a lady. And this guy could draw. Years of academy training in Rotterdam. He knew how to put a face together. But knowing how to draw a hand doesn't tell you what to do with it. He talked about getting stuck on things like the woman's knees. Now try following one of her arms. You catch an outline, then it disappears into the paint. But those eyes and teeth keep bringing you back. So he'd paint her in. Scrape whole sections off. And then, you know, paint her in again. Same canvas. Nearly two years. He said he wasn't after perfection. He wanted to see how far he could go. Looks like he just threw it on there. But those are a lot of second thoughts. At one point he took it off the stretcher and just gave up. A friend encouraged him to go back to it. So back she went. Then you think, how the hell does he know when it's finished? Years later, somebody actually asked him that. "I just stop, you know." Alright, well… that clears that up. When this one finally left for the gallery, a critic wrote that she escaped by truck from her creator. This is Woman One. There are six in the numbered series. When he made this, de Kooning was already famous for his abstract paintings. Some of his fellow painters thought going back to recognizable things was a step backward. On opening night, Jackson Pollock reportedly told him, "Bill, you betrayed it. You're doing the figure, you're still doing the same goddamn thing." Apparently, there were rules. Other people saw the painting as violent. Misogynistic. And MoMA? They bought it. Their own committee found it "quite frightening." But they also liked the color.
