Le Voyage dans la Lune

Georges Méliès, 1902 · Gallery 501 · Floor 5

The magician isn't impressed. December, 1895. Georges Méliès is in a basement under a café in Paris. The Lumières are demonstrating their new machine. On the screen, a photograph of a square in Lyon. Georges runs his own theater. He remembers complaining to the man next to him. He's been doing this for ten years. Then the photograph moves. Now he wants the machine. Ten thousand francs. Their father, Antoine Lumière, says nope. So he gets equipment from England. Builds a glass studio in his garden. A greenhouse with a stage in it. And in 1902, he makes this. A Trip to the Moon. Fourteen minutes. Those early Lumière films ran under one. Whatever part you've walked in on, stay for the cannon. Women in sailor suits load the capsule. Dancers from the Châtelet theater. Then the moon comes toward you. That's an actor, his body hidden in black velvet, sitting on a chair they pull toward the camera. They're moving the moon. The capsule in the eye? Stop filming. Put it there. Start again. Cut the pieces together. Same with the moon creatures. Acrobats from the Folies Bergère. Hit one with an umbrella. Smoke. Gone. The actor has time to leave. You don't see that part. That's why they call him the father of special effects. The film's a hit. Other companies copy it and sell it themselves. Including Thomas Edison's. Yeah. The light bulb guy. Méliès gets the fame. They get paid. He keeps going, but bigger companies take over, and by 1913 he's stopped. He loses the studio. Film stock gets sold for recycling. Some ends up in shoe heels. By 1925, he's selling toys and candy at the Montparnasse station with his wife, Jehanne. She used to be in his movies. So was he. The guy running the expedition up there. Big white beard. Then a film journalist finds him. People dig up the films, throw him a gala. France gives him the Legion of Honor. And the moon? The actor in the velvet. Capsule in his eye. That's him too.
