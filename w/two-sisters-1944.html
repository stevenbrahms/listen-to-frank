Two Sisters

John D. Graham, 1944 · Gallery 522 · Floor 5

So, John Graham said his father was Jupiter. You know. The god. So the cavalry officer part sounds pretty reasonable after that. Served under the Tsar. Went to prison after the Revolution. Some of that checks out. Sharing a cell with the Tsar himself? That’s another Graham story. Anyway, he gets to New York, already in his thirties, and starts learning to paint. Writes a book about art. Younger painters listen to him. And he’s got an eye. A decorating firm asks him to put a show together, and he hangs Picasso, Matisse and Braque alongside some Americans hardly anybody knows yet. De Kooning. Lee Krasner. Jackson Pollock. De Kooning later gives Graham the credit for discovering Pollock. Very clear about it. Graham did it. But as those painters move toward abstraction, Graham heads back toward Raphael. And starts calling Picasso a fraud. Same Picasso he put in the show. Some old friends are startled. They’d spent years listening to this guy explain modern art. Two years after that show, he paints these women. Look at them. Pearls, jewels, velvet. All dressed for a Renaissance portrait, except nobody’s quite got their top on. He spends all this time on that jewelled collar on the right, then the cloth beside it gets a few quick strokes of blue and yellow. Parts of the hands are still pencil. And the woman on the left’s holding a pigeon. Look just under its head. There’s another beak drawn in. Bigger than the painted one. He left it. Now the eyes. Both women. Crossed. Graham said that gave his paintings more tension than Raphael’s. But then, he said, he lived in a more tense age. Raphael had it easy, I guess.
