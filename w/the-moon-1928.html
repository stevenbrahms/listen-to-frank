The Moon

Tarsila do Amaral, 1928

This painting is so simple. Just five things. A yellow moon. Some clouds. Green hills. A stream. A cactus. Like you could draw it from memory after seeing it once. Look at the cactus again. A round little body, a head, something like an arm. It’s standing there the way a person stands at a railing, looking up at the moon. Like she grew somebody out of the ground. Tarsila do Amaral. She’s been going back and forth between Brazil and Paris, studying with French painters. Learning how to turn a body or a landscape into a few simple shapes. She called it her military service in Cubism. Then, she paints her husband a birthday present. A figure with a tiny head and an enormous foot. And next to him, a cactus. They call it Abaporu. The name means the man who eats human flesh. Her husband’s a writer, Oswald de Andrade. And he somehow gets a whole manifesto out of his birthday present. The idea is that Brazil should eat European culture. Ya know, digest it. And then make something of its own. So... you can learn from Paris. You just don’t have to spend the rest of your life painting Paris. That idea, and her painting, become a whole movement. And this painting, The Moon, is from the same year. Same cactus, even. A Brazilian landscape where a cactus looks like it’s got something on its mind. Back in Brazil, people just call her Tarsila. Everybody there knows who you mean.
