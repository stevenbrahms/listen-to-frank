The Dream

Henri Rousseau, 1910

So people had questions about the couch. A naked lady, on a red velvet couch. Ya know, the fancy kind you’d have in a Paris living room. Rousseau’s put it in a jungle. Plants coming up around the arms. Lotus flowers. An elephant peeking out of the leaves. Two lions down below. One’s looking straight at you. And that figure back there, almost lost in the dark. The snake charmer. When Rousseau first shows it, he puts a little poem with it. The woman’s called Yadwigha. She’s fallen asleep, and in her dream she hears the snake charmer’s music. But a critic still wants to know what the furniture’s doing there. So Rousseau writes back. She’s asleep on the couch, dreaming she’s been carried into the forest. That’s why he’s put the couch in. Seems perfectly reasonable to him. Okay, so... we’re inside her dream. Couch and all. Except, look at her eyes. Wide open. Arm stretched out toward the music. Asleep, he says. Anyway, so Rousseau’s been showing at that same exhibition for years, and people laugh. So he asks his poet friend Apollinaire to defend him in print. Avenge him, basically. And Apollinaire does. There’s beauty here, he writes, no arguing with it. Nobody’s going to laugh this year, he says. Ask the painters. Anyway, the jungle’s from the glasshouses in Paris. He said being among those strange plants felt like entering a dream. Then he comes home and paints all this, leaf by leaf. Somebody once counted more than fifty different greens in here. And that name, Yadwigha. The story goes she was a Polish girl he’d loved when he was young. So there may be an old love in this dream, too. He’s an old man by now. She’s young. Stretched out with the lions and the snake, looking pretty comfortable. This is his last big painting. A few months later, he’s dead. And she’s still on that couch. Eyes open. Listening.
