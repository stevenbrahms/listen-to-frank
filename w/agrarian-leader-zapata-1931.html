Agrarian Leader Zapata

Diego Rivera, 1931

Okay, look at that horse first. Big, white, gorgeous. And there’s a bit of Italy in it. Rivera borrowed the idea from a battle painting from the fourteen hundreds. A guy named Uccello. Ya know, the kind of horse you give a war captain. So look who’s got the reins in this one. That guy in sandals. Emiliano Zapata. He led peasants in the Mexican Revolution, fighting to take land back from wealthy estates and return it to the people working it. Tierra y libertad. Land and liberty. In photos, Zapata’s a charro. Tight pants, a vest with silver on it, the big hat. A serious horseman, and he dressed like one. Rivera puts him in plain white farm clothes. On foot. Holding a machete. The thing you cut sugarcane with. And behind him, we got an army of garden tools. Now look down by his feet. The dead man. Fancy riding boots. A hacienda owner. That white horse was his. So... Zapata took it off him. Rivera took the look of it off Uccello. Same move, twice. And Rivera paints this thing right inside MoMA. It’s 1931. The museum’s barely two years old. They want to show his murals. He’s the big Mexican muralist, but his stuff is literally attached to buildings. Ya know... little problem. They’re frescoes. You paint into wet plaster, and the color becomes part of the wall as it dries. So Rivera makes a wall that can travel. Cement and steel, nearly eight feet tall. They give him an empty gallery to work in. One of his supporters is this rich lady Abby Rockefeller. She helped found the place. So we got an outspoken Communist painting a dead estate owner. The rich lady’s a fan. The show sets an attendance record. Then the Rockefellers hire him for a giant mural in the lobby at Rockefeller Center. Rivera paints Lenin into it. Nelson Rockefeller, Abby’s son, asks him to take Lenin out. Rivera refuses. So they cover it up. Then they have it smashed off the wall. Anyway, six years later, MoMA buys this one. With a fund in Abby Rockefeller’s name.
