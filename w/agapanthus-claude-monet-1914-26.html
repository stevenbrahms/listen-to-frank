Agapanthus

Claude Monet, 1914-26 · Gallery 515 · Floor 5

Look at those stems. Long, bending arcs. Little purple flowers at the ends. And all that green, with blue coming through it. Agapanthus. Monet planted them along his pond at Giverny. In his thirties, he was out making those quick, bright landscapes that helped get Impressionism going. Now he's in his seventies. He's got the garden. Six full-time gardeners. But his wife has died. He's barely painting. Then he gets going again. Huge canvases. The pond. Everything growing along the banks. This one's taller than you are. And his eyes are going. Cataracts. He tells an interviewer he's choosing his paints by the labels on the tubes. Monet. Having to check which one's blue. Now look how much blue he's put in here. One of his closest friends is Georges Clemenceau. France's prime minister at the end of the First World War. The day after the Armistice, Monet writes to him. He wants to give France two big paintings. His only way, he says, of taking part in the victory. Two paintings becomes twenty-two. They end up in two oval rooms in Paris. This one stays at Giverny. His son keeps it. Now, Clemenceau also trained as a doctor. And Monet's been told he needs surgery on those eyes. He's afraid it'll blind him. So he keeps putting it off. Then Clemenceau writes him a letter. Says Monet taught him to understand light. "My eyes need your color." When Monet dies, they put a black cloth over the coffin. Clemenceau sees it. "No black for Monet." He finds an old cloth in the house to use instead. The colors of forget-me-nots. Periwinkles. Hydrangeas.
