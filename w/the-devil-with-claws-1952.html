The Devil with Claws

Germaine Richier, 1952

Follow the wire. From the creature's hand, through that open space in its body, down to its foot. There's one on the other side too. Real wire. Pulled tight. You might not notice it at first. Now try looking at anything else. Feels like the thing's caught. Some kind of trap. But those wires also look like plumb lines. Sculptors use them to check whether a figure's standing straight. Almost like Germaine Richier left her tools in the sculpture. She comes out of the old Rodin tradition. A devout Catholic. A few years after making this, she'll be the first living woman to have a show at France's national museum of modern art. But just before this, she's making something for a church in the French Alps. A crucifix. Her model's a very skinny man named Lyrot. She makes Christ and the cross one piece. And where you'd expect a face, there's almost nothing. Knots. Like an old branch. Traditionalist Catholics call it blasphemy. The bishop has it taken out of the church. It really gets to her. The next year, she's working on this creature. And she brings Lyrot back. Same man who posed for Christ. She calls this one The Devil. Quite a range. Now look at what she's done with his body. Bent over. Reaching two ways at once. Is he pulling against the wires? Or would he fall over without them? You can't quite work it out. Find the arm hanging down. That hook coming off the elbow. Her husband later gives the sculpture a different name, after those claws. And the crucifix? They did put it back over the altar. Ten years after she died.
