The Moroccans

Henri Matisse, 1916 · Gallery 506 · Floor 5

Kind of a strange one… coming from the color guy. Look at all that black. A big empty field of it, right through the middle. Matisse spent two winters in Tangier, in Morocco. He was always going south for the light. And down there, it’s brutal. Then, back in France, he paints what he remembers. Up on the left, a white dome behind a balcony. Over on the right, all that dusty pink, and a man in a white turban, with his back to us. Now, those green lumps down below. They kinda look like melons? Or are they bowed backs and heads of guys praying? Matisse said melons. Watermelons and gourds. Other people see men praying. You can flip between them all day. He carried the idea around for years. Sketched it in a letter home to his wife, from Tangier. By the time he finishes it, he’s in a studio outside Paris. And out east, at a place called Verdun, France and Germany are in the longest battle of the First World War. They’re dying by the thousands. And there’s all that black. Easy to read it as gloom. But that’s not how Matisse explained it. Thirty-odd years later, he said this was where his blacks began. Black didn’t have to mean shadow. Or night. He said the big black in his Moroccans glowed as much as any color in it. He’d painted light with it. Even the light of Tangier.
