Ingenio

Cameron Rowland, 2023

At first glance, this looks like a huge rusted wok. Or something a guy in Brooklyn swears he's gonna turn into a fire pit. It's a sugar kettle. Nineteenth century. Cast iron. Made in England, used in Louisiana. Sixteen hundred pounds. Look at that crack down the side. From the rim down into the belly. Cameron Rowland calls this Ingenio. That's what a colonial sugar mill was called in Spanish. The engine. And this was one of its parts. Once you cut sugarcane, it starts going bad. You've got to process it fast. So the mill runs sixteen, eighteen hours a day. Enslaved Black people grow the cane, cut it, crush it, then boil the juice in a bunch of kettles like this one. All that work. So Rowland writes the caption for this. It tells you all of that. And how the work was enforced. It quotes this historian, C. L. R. James. James describes enslaved people getting the whip more reliably than they got their food. Then comes the last line. "Sugar was made this way for four hundred years." Sugar has incredible PR. Think about it. Sugar is cupcakes. Your aunt asking if you want one lump or two. But you definitely don't picture this thing. Or the people made to work around it. Now, here's how Rowland ended up with it. While doing research, Rowland comes across a business outside Atlanta selling old sugar kettles. For people's gardens. Fountains. Fire pits. On the website, some of the owners pose beside them in their yards. So Rowland buys this one. Out of that trade. Same kettle. Different caption.
