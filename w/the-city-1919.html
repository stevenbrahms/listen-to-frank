The City

Fernand Léger, 1919 · Gallery 505 · Floor 5

See that big B, over on the left? No word attached. And that big purple pole over to the right? Right in the way. Cities do that. Fernand Léger paints this in 1919, just after the First World War. Machines are his thing. Back in the war, in the Argonne, he notices sunlight on the open back of a seventy-five-millimeter cannon. He calls it "the magic of light on the white metal." He's looking at a gun. And noticing how beautiful it is. Later, he gets gassed and ends up in hospital. Now come back to what he paints afterward. The City. Billboards, scaffolding, a staircase. Color coming at you from every direction. Full volume. Then you've got those two people down by the stairs. Almost everything else in here is blocks of color and hard edges. But these two are the soft part. Smudgy. Kind of in the shadows. Even that blue guy on the billboard, over on the right, is painted sharper than either of them. And there's no horizon here. No nice clear view of the whole place. You get a staircase, a face, a bit of scaffolding. Then something else gets in the way. A sign, shouting half a word at you. And remember where he's just been. Everything camouflaged. Everything trying not to be seen. "Four years without colour," he called it.
