Attica Series Desk

Cameron Rowland, 2016

You've used this desk. Not this exact one. But this desk. Black steel. Fake-wood top. The color that's not really a color. Waiting rooms. Schools with tight budgets. You probably never once wondered who made it. In 2025, a big art magazine ranked the best artworks of this century so far. Number one: this desk. An incarcerated person at Attica made it. That's not metaphor. It comes out of a catalog called Corcraft, the sales arm of New York's prison system. The product line is literally the Attica Series. Corcraft's name, not the artist's. Attica. September 9th, 1971. Nearly thirteen hundred prisoners take the yard. A twenty-one-year-old named L.D. Barkley reads their statement to the news cameras. "We are men. We are not beasts." First practical proposal on their list: the state minimum wage for prison labor. "STOP SLAVE LABOR." Capital letters. Four days. Then helicopters, tear gas, state police shooting. Thirty-nine people die, hostages included. The state's bullets, every one. Barkley is one of the dead. He was days from getting out. 2016. An artist named Cameron Rowland puts this desk in a show in New York. Rowland's method: buy the thing, change nothing, write the paperwork. But Corcraft won't sell to an artist. Only government, schools, courts, cops, some nonprofits. The gallery's a nonprofit. It signs up. Its customer number is the name of the show. 91020000. The listed pay that year for the men building these desks: ten cents to a dollar fourteen an hour. Nothing on the desk got touched. No scratch, no paint. Just moved. Prison to museum. Rowland won't sell it. MoMA rents it, five years at a time, for exactly what it costs in the prison catalog. MoMA can't own it. Rowland's essay for that show opens with the Thirteenth Amendment, word for word. The one that ended slavery. There's an exception in it. "Except as a punishment for crime."
