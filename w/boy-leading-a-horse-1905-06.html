BOY LEADING A HORSE

PABLO PICASSO, 1905-06

His right hand. Closed in a fist, right at the horse's chest. Now look for what's in it. Nothing. No rope, no bridle, no halter. Check the whole horse. Not one piece of leather on that animal. It follows him anyway. People love that part. Fair enough. It's the wrong mystery. The real one is the land they're walking through. No road. No water. No buildings, no trees, no people. Pink dirt, a couple of low hills, a sky doing the bare minimum. An old MoMA catalogue calls it "a kind of nonenvironment." Fancy way of saying there's nothing out there. Paris, winter into 1906. Picasso is planning something huge. A watering place. Horses drinking, riders, boys all over. And walking straight through the middle of it, this kid, leading a horse down to the water. He works it out over and over. Gouache, watercolor, a drawing, a little drypoint. Then he drops it. The big picture never gets made. But this kid he keeps. Cuts him out of the crowd and gives him a canvas over seven feet tall, all to himself. A real face. Calm. Classical. Clock that face. The rest of this room is what happens to it. And everything else from the plan is missing. The riders, gone. The other boys, gone. The water this horse was walking to, gone. Maybe that's why the place reads like nowhere. You're looking at the middle of a picture that doesn't exist. One more thing. The plan survived. This museum owns it. A drypoint. The plate's barely bigger than a postcard. Every rider, every horse, this same boy. The whole plan fits in your hand. The boy Picasso kept needed seven feet.
