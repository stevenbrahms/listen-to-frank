Vir Heroicus Sublimis

Barnett Newman, 1950 · Gallery 402 · Floor 4

Damn. Look how big this thing is. Now you gotta get a little closer. Closer than feels comfortable. That’s what Barnett Newman wanted. When he first showed it, it was the biggest painting he’d ever made. He pinned a typed note to the wall saying people tend to stand back from big pictures. These were meant to be seen close. He compared it to meeting another person. This one just happens to be eighteen feet wide. Vir Heroicus Sublimis. Man, heroic and sublime. Think less guy on a horse. More you, standing here. Almost nobody came to the show. Six years later, it’s in a museum show in Minneapolis. A critic calls it the most asinine thing there. Damn near eighteen feet across, he writes, and painted flat red. Newman writes back. Takes issue with the swearing. No need to swear at the damn size. The exact size is right there in the catalogue. Now, about that flat red. Find the white line, about a quarter of the way in. Newman called these “zips.” That one’s crisp, almost a gap between two sheets of red. The wider, darker one toward the right seems to sink into it. And the thin orange ones toward the edges go soft. A young collector, a guy named Ben Heller, didn’t see much in any of this at first. Pollock sent him to Newman’s studio, and he couldn’t get out fast enough. On the street, he tells his wife it’s the emperor’s clothes. Nothing there. But something bothers him enough that he goes back. By the third visit, he thinks Newman’s a great painter. He buys this. His family’s in the cloth business. His father takes a look. “My God, it’s like one dye lot.” A couple of years later, Heller’s calling Newman probably the biggest influence on the younger painters coming up. Eventually, MoMA buys five paintings from Heller. But the committee won’t pay for this one. So he gives it to them. And according to Heller, Newman’s furious. He had a buyer. Heller says that wasn’t the point. It belongs here.
