UNTITLED

MARK ROTHKO, 1969–1970

Almost all the color is gone. Black on top. Gray underneath. One hard line where they meet. And a thin white border around the whole thing, like somebody finally told Rothko where the edge was. That border is new. Here’s what changed. April 1968. Doctors find an aneurysm in his aorta. Then they give a man who’s spent his life making paintings bigger than people one rule: nothing taller than three feet. That’s not medical advice. That’s basically a sentence. And for a while, he listens. He turns toward smaller work. A lot of it on paper. Acrylic instead of the old, slow layers of oil. But not completely. This thing is six and a half feet tall. Rothko was never great with instructions. And the color keeps dropping out. Black over gray. Gray after gray. He made these in a converted carriage house on East Sixty-Ninth Street. There was a huge skylight overhead, and Rothko had a parachute rigged beneath it so he could pull it back and forth and control the daylight. Of course he did. Even now, the light had to behave. People have been trying to explain these paintings ever since. Moonscapes, because it’s 1969 and everybody’s looking at the moon. Endings, because everybody knows what happened next. Rothko said almost nothing. Here’s what we actually know. On the morning of February 25, 1970, his Seagram paintings arrived at the Tate in London. The ones he made for that restaurant, walked away from, then eventually gave to the museum. That same morning, back in the studio, his assistant found him. Rothko had taken his own life. He was sixty-six. But nobody gets to turn this into a suicide note. Rothko never called it that. So just look at what’s actually here. The gray isn’t empty. It’s scrubbed, streaked, restless. The black above it barely moves. And between them, one blunt line. A horizon, maybe. If you need it to be. He didn’t live to see the Rothko Chapel open. He didn’t see the Tate room built around those murals. But you’re standing in the thing he wanted. So, stay as long as you need.
