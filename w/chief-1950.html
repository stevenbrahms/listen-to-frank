Chief

Franz Kline, 1950

For years, Franz Kline’s been making these tiny drawings. On little scraps of paper. Chairs, streets, bridges. Stuff you could fold up and lose in your pocket. In the late forties, he’s over at Willem de Kooning’s studio. Another painter. De Kooning’s got a projector. A Bell-Opticon. They try one of Kline’s drawings. A black rocking chair, four inches by five. Put it up on the wall. And suddenly, it’s enormous. Those little chair legs start looking like girders. And the chair? You can’t find it. Just big black shapes. It takes a while, but over the next year or so, Kline’s paintings get large. Big, black and white. That’s what he’s known for. This one’s five feet by six. Brushes as wide as your hand. Black paint flying across the canvas. Like he walked up, took a few enormous swings, and went out for lunch. But actually, he usually starts with a small drawing. Sometimes a page torn out of the phone book. Then slowly builds it up on the canvas. Black. Then white. More black. More white. Because the white’s painted too. “I paint the white as well as the black,” he said, “and the white is just as important.” Now look at those narrow white lanes squeezing through. The black’s pushing in. The white’s pushing back. Two paintings shoving each other around. Then there’s the name. Chief. That was a locomotive Kline remembered from when he was a kid in Pennsylvania. And growing up there wasn’t easy. His father killed himself when Kline was seven. A couple of years later, he was sent away to a school for fatherless boys, and he didn’t come home until he was fifteen. By then, his mother had married a foreman on the railroad. So the tracks, the bridges, the trains, all that big black machinery. That’s the world he’d come home to. Now look at those two black bars up top. See how they run right off the edge? Like something just tore past you. A lot of people look at these and see machinery. But Kline said he wasn’t painting what he saw. He was painting, in his words, “the feelings aroused in me by that looking.”
