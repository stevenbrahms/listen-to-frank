Sackcloth 1953

Alberto Burri, 1953

Alright, find that long white thread near the top. Coming off the frayed patch. Wandering out to the left. Keep that in mind. During the war, Alberto Burri's a doctor in the Italian army. The British capture him in Tunisia. He ends up, of all places, in a prisoner-of-war camp. In Texas. They won't let him practice medicine. So he starts painting. Every day, he said. Helped keep his mind off the war. Then supplies get cut. He gets empty burlap sacks from the mess hall and paints on those. After the war, he goes home to Italy. Gives up medicine. But a few years later, he's back on the sacks, cutting into them. Sewing pieces together. Leaving others torn. MoMA's list has no paint on it. Burlap. And thread. The cloth gives him the color. The stitches make the lines. And where there's a hole, he leaves it. Something dark showing through. Now, a doctor sewing up torn material, with that loose thread hanging off it. You can see where the critics went. Stitches. Wounds. Burri wouldn't go along with that. "Words are no help to me," he said. MoMA buys this one the year after he makes it. Back in Italy, it's a harder sell. Rome's national modern art gallery hangs one of his big sack paintings. A senator wants to know what the state paid for it. Old, dirty, torn packing cloth. He raises this in parliament. Somebody has to answer a parliamentary question about a sack. The answer is nothing. Burri gave it to them. Now look back at those holes. What's behind those holes? Canvas. In Texas, the sack was what he had to paint on. Here, he's got canvas. He puts the sacks over it.
