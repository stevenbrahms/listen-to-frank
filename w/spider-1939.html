Spider

Alexander Calder, 1939 · Gallery 522 · Floor 5

Watch those little black shapes for a minute. It doesn’t move on schedule. You gotta wait. If somebody walks past, stirs the air, you might catch it. One piece lifts, another drops. Nearly seven feet tall, all black. On one side, that big rounded triangle of aluminum. On the other, all those curved arms. The low ones end in little black teardrops. Look like legs, once you’ve seen it. Alexander Calder calls this one Spider. One big shape balanced against a lot of little ones. Now go back to October 1930. Paris. He’s visiting the painter Piet Mondrian in his studio. Light coming in from both sides, colored cardboard rectangles pinned all over the wall. And Calder suggests making the rectangles move. Mondrian looks at him, dead serious, and says, no, not necessary. My painting is already very fast. A painting of rectangles. Very fast. Okay, sure. Anyway, Calder goes home and tries abstract painting for a couple of weeks, then gets back to making things. By the following autumn, he’s using cranks and little electric motors. Marcel Duchamp comes by and gives them a name. Mobile. In French, something that moves. Also, a motive. The name stuck. By February, Calder’s showing them in Paris, and the way he tells it, everybody’s enthusiastic. Except Mondrian. Says they don’t move fast enough. So Calder speeds them up. Still too slow. So he offers to build one especially fast, to please him. Nope. That wouldn’t be fast enough either. Because the whole thing ought to be still. Now come back to this one. Made seven years later. Nobody touches it. No crank. No little electric motor. Just those shapes, balanced in the air. He lets the room run it.
