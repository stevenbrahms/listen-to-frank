The Starry Night

Vincent van Gogh, 1889 · Gallery 501 · Floor 5

Alright, so find the big white star. Left of the middle. Just to the right of that dark tree. The one with all that light around it. That’s the one he wrote his brother about. You know this one. Vincent van Gogh. Dutch guy. In about ten years, he makes more than two thousand paintings and drawings. Hardly sells anything. Now, he’s on every mug and umbrella in the gift shop. When he makes this, it’s June, 1889. He’s thirty-six. And a patient in an asylum in the south of France. Checked himself in that May. There are iron bars across his bedroom window. Outside, a wheat field and some hills beyond that. One morning, way before the sunrise, he looks out. And there’s this light. He writes to his brother Theo about “the morning star, which looked very big.” That’s actually Venus. The planet. Anyway, they’ve given him a spare room to work in. And that’s where he paints this. A night sky, painted during the day. So, now look at the town. He couldn’t see that town from his window. But he puts it there. And the church steeple looks more like something back in Holland. So… a little bit of home ends up in this French landscape. And that big dark tree up front? He brings it much closer. See how it rises. Almost like a flame. Then look at the blue between the stars. Those long curls are made of little strokes. One after another, following the bend. Around each star, more strokes. Rings of white and yellow. The light looks like it’s kinda spreading. And down below, those houses sit there. A few little windows lit. The whole sky seems to be rolling over them. He said the night was more alive, more richly colored than the day. You can see what he means. Painting how the night feels, not just how it looks. In a way, that’s a lot of where modern painting kinda starts. So in September, he sends the painting to Theo in Paris. But he has doubts. Calls the arrangement an exaggeration. So go back to that white light. The one he actually saw from his window before dawn. The one that looked very big. That November, he writes to a painter friend. Said he thinks he’s made the stars too big. “Another failure.”
