Bird-Head

Max Ernst, 1934-35 · Gallery 520 · Floor 5

This one’s got a face. Big flat square head. Two round eyes. A long nose. And up top, a little bird’s head sticking its beak out. Little stubby legs. It looks kind of friendly. Like something a kid would draw if you said, draw a bird, but also make him a paper bag guy. Max Ernst made it. A big name in Dada, and then in Surrealism. And the way Ernst tells it, he’s a teenager when his pet cockatoo dies in the night. Next morning, his father tells him his baby sister’s been born. So follow the teenage logic here. Bird gone. Baby arrives. Same night. So the baby’s taken the bird’s life. That’s obviously not how anything works. Doesn’t matter. The idea sticks. He blamed the baby, by the way. His own sister. For the bird. And he writes all this down himself, as a grown man. In the third person. Like somebody ought to look into this Max Ernst character. By then, he’s already invented another version of himself. A bird, obviously. Loplop. Official title: the Bird Superior. And in that same write-up, he says he identified himself with Loplop. Voluntarily. So his own imaginary bird outranked him. And for years, Loplop’s all over his work. Presenting pictures. Introducing people. A feathered master of ceremonies. Sometimes he’s a big flat rectangle with a bird’s head poking over the top and little feet underneath. Now look at the bronze again. There’s that rectangle. Those feet. The bird up top. The museum thinks it also looks a lot like a kind of plank mask made by the Toussian people of Burkina Faso. Flat. Rectangular. With carved hornbill heads. Another bird. Anyway, Ernst was living in Paris then, collecting African art. There’s no record he ever studied one. But a flat rectangle with a bird on top? Maybe it’s Loplop, holding still for once.
