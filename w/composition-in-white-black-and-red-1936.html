Composition in White, Black, and Red

Piet Mondrian, 1936 · Gallery 512 · Floor 5

So, Mondrian sells this one to another painter. This guy George Morris who’s got some Old New York money. Morris is in Paris for a show he’s in. Mondrian turns up at the opening, and Morris visits his studio. And he buys this one before it’s even finished. It’s for the Museum of Modern Art, which isn’t even seven years old. The money comes from a committee of mostly young supporters. George Gershwin’s on it. Now look at what they bought. One red bar. Along the bottom edge, on the right. Thin. Stubborn. No yellow. No blue. Just above it, small, in the same red: P M, thirty-six. More than three feet of canvas. And look at what that little red bar has to balance. All that white. Mondrian called art a substitute. Basically, something you need while life’s still out of whack. Once life gets its act together, you wouldn’t need a painting of it. You’d be living in it. The next summer, the New York Times Magazine runs this painting in a story on where abstract art is heading. The next day, the Nazis open Degenerate Art in Munich. Confiscated paintings. Insults on the walls. Built so you point and laugh. Mondrian’s in it. Dutch guy. They thought he was German. Both paintings they hung there were later destroyed. This one was already in New York. He shows up about four years after it.
