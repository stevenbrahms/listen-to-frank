Les Demoiselles d'Avignon

Pablo Picasso, 1907 · Gallery 502 · Floor 5

This painting has a seam. Look at the two women in the middle, with their arms behind their heads. Those faces could almost be statues. Then go right. Masks. Slashes across the faces. It’s like the painting’s changed its mind halfway through. Picasso’s twenty-five. He’s painting five women in a brothel. Avignon Street, back in Barcelona. His name for it was The Brothel of Avignon. A writer friend later tidies that up to The Young Ladies of Avignon. Anyway, in the early sketches, there are men in here too. A sailor at a table, and a medical student coming in carrying a skull. Neither man makes it into the painting. So now it’s you the women are facing. His painter friend Georges Braque sees it in the studio. The story goes, he says it’s like being made to drink kerosene. Within a year, Braque paints his own big nude. Before long, the two of them are working out Cubism together. Now look at the woman crouching down on the right. Her back’s turned, but her face comes straight around at you. She didn’t always look like that. Picasso painted over both faces on the right. X-rays show what was there before. A small nose. An ear. Beneath the bridge of the crouching woman’s nose, the X-ray picks up an eye from an earlier face. Years later, Picasso talks about a museum he visited in Paris, right in the middle of working on this. Masks from Africa and the Pacific, brought there through the colonies. He remembers the smell. Says it caught him by the throat. But he stayed. To him, those masks were weapons. Against spirits. He called this his first exorcism.
