The Vertigo of Eros

The Vertigo of Eros by Roberto Matta, 1944, is in Gallery 522 on Floor 5 at MoMA. Frank's free, unofficial audio guide tells its story in 1 min 43 sec.

Roberto Matta, 1944 · Gallery 522 · Floor 5

Alright, so. Twin boys. Born in New York. In June. Their father’s Roberto Matta. A painter from Chile, in his early thirties. His parents had wanted something practical. So… architecture. He gets to Paris and lands a job with Le Corbusier. That’s about as good as it gets. But he spends less and less time at the office. More and more with the Surrealists. Pretty soon, he’s one of the youngest in the group. Then comes the war. He moves to New York. Young American painters start coming around his studio. Motherwell. Pollock. He gets them drawing. Let the hand move. See what turns up. And they ran a long way with that. Up to then, Matta’s paintings had mostly been small. Then he makes this. Wider than a couch. He’s only made three big paintings by this point. This is one. Now, find somewhere to stand. A floor. A horizon. Anything. Like a galaxy blew apart and the dust is still hanging there. He calls it The Vertigo of Eros. Eros, for Freud, is the pull toward life. Here, it seems to knock you a little off-balance. Matta calls these pictures inscapes. He thinks you can look inside yourself. So… this is the view. Later, he says he’d spent that whole stretch looking inward. Then realized there was a horrible crisis happening outside. “My vision of myself,” he said, “was becoming blind.” The year he paints this, the twins turn one. By summer, he and their mother have split up. One of those boys is Gordon Matta-Clark. He grows up. Studies architecture. Then gets famous for cutting buildings open. A whole house. Right in half.
