Opus 217. Against the Enamel of a Background Rhythmic with Beats and Angles, Tones, and Tints, Portrait of M. Félix Fénéon in 1890

Opus 217. Against the Enamel of a Background Rhythmic with Beats and Angles, Tones, and Tints, Portrait of M. Félix Fénéon in 1890 by Paul Signac, 1890, is in Gallery 501 on Floor 5 at MoMA. Frank's free, unofficial audio guide tells its story in 2 min 00 sec.

Paul Signac, 1890 · Gallery 501 · Floor 5

See that elegant guy holding out the flower? Four years after this, the police find detonators in his desk. At the French Ministry of War. Where he works. That's Félix Fénéon. Chief clerk. Art critic. Anarchist. He's one of the first people who understands what Seurat and Signac are doing with all those little dots. Defends them. Even gives the movement its name. Neo-Impressionism. Then, in 1894, a bomb goes off at a Paris restaurant called Foyot. A few weeks later, Fénéon gets arrested. Ends up on trial with a roomful of other suspected anarchists. You'd think he might keep his head down for a minute. Then the judge brings up something he was seen doing. Fénéon, talking to an anarchist. Behind a gas lamp. Fénéon has a question about that. Which side of a gas lamp, your honor, is 'its' behind? In the end, the jury acquits him. Nobody ever proves he planted the restaurant bomb. Anyway, come back to the flower. Signac's painting his friend here. And he gives him quite a background. Spirals. Stars. Bands of color shooting off in every direction. Then this incredibly composed man in front of it. Hat and cane in one hand. A little flower in the other. Looks like he's producing it out of thin air. Get closer, though. The jacket, the face, all that spinning color. Little separate touches of paint. The whole painting is made with the technique this guy helped people understand. Signac gives him the portrait. There's just one problem. When Signac offered to paint him, Fénéon had one request. Full face. Looking straight out at you. Look at him. Signac turned him sideways. The year before he dies, Fénéon writes to a friend that it's the least successful thing Signac ever painted. In 1890, he says, Signac didn't know him well enough yet. But he keeps it the rest of his life.
