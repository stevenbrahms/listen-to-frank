Tomahawk Nolan

John Chamberlain, 1965 · Gallery 408 · Floor 4

Car parts. Cut up, bent, crushed, welded. And the longer you look, the less it looks like a wreck. It starts getting kinda elegant on you. Annoyingly elegant. John Chamberlain used to do hair. Seriously. He was a hairdresser in Chicago. By the late fifties, he's making sculpture. Alright, so he's staying at the painter Larry Rivers's place out on Long Island. There's this old Ford rusting in the yard. It's Larry's Ford, by the way. So John just takes the fenders off. Then he lays them on the ground and just drives over them. With his own car, a few times. To 'rearrange' the shape, as he put it. Then he welds them together with rods. That one's called Shortstop. His first sculpture using car parts. Turns out, he's found his material. And think about the painters he admires. De Kooning. Kline. Those big, swinging brushstrokes. Well, he's found a way to make one stand up. Out of car metal. That's the line on him, anyway. And the car parts come in color. Which was kind of a no-no back then, color on a sculpture. Chamberlain didn't see the problem. So look at this one. It's called Tomahawk Nolan. That big yellow piece up top, pitched like a roof. The blue crumple underneath. And down at the bottom, that red fold. Look at where one piece pushes into another. Where it buckles. Where it curls back out. Those folds look almost soft. Like clothes dropped on a chair. Chamberlain had his own version. Throw your towels around the bathroom. Look at your bed, all crumpled up. Doesn't look that different from his work. And remember the hair. He said he probably learned about resistance cutting hair. How much a thing pushes back. Then he found car metal. Said it had a right amount of pushback.
