Nude with Joined Hands

Pablo Picasso, 1906 · Gallery 502 · Floor 5

So Picasso takes a woman and gives her a face about two thousand years old. She’s probably his girlfriend, Fernande. Look at the rest of her. Peachy skin, against all that pink. A bent knee. Hands clasped together. You can feel the weight of her. Then you get to the face. Flat. Almond eyes. A little shut mouth. Like somebody carved it. Now, he’s been having trouble with another woman’s face. Gertrude Stein. She’s a writer, and one of his early collectors. She’s been coming to his studio all winter, sitting for a portrait. Eighty or ninety times, according to her. And he keeps working at it. Until one day, he just paints the whole head out. “I can’t see you any longer when I look.” Imagine hearing that after ninety appointments. Around this time, he sees ancient Iberian sculpture at the Louvre. Faces from Spain, more than two thousand years old. Those almond eyes. Those little mouths. Then he and Fernande go up to Gósol, a village in the Pyrenees. And up there, the faces he paints start looking like this. Next year, faces like these turn up in Les Demoiselles d’Avignon. But come back to her. That warm, heavy body. And a face that looks like somebody dug it out of the ground. By the end of the summer, he’s back in Paris, working on Stein’s portrait again. Only now, he doesn’t need her there. He finishes the face from memory. Hard. Simplified. People tell him it doesn’t look like her. “She will,” he says. Gertrude’s satisfied. Says, for her, it is her. She hangs it at home. Eventually, it ends up at the Met, right across town. And this one? She and her brother Leo get it straight from Picasso. Years later, they divide up their collection. She keeps this one. And she keeps it until she dies. About forty years. Picasso spent months trying to work out what to do with a face. Gertrude took home both answers.
