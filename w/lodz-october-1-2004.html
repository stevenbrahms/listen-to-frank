Łódź, October 1, 2004

Christopher Williams, 2004 · Gallery 203 · Floor 2

So, Christopher Williams doesn't take his own pictures. He hires a commercial photographer. Somebody who shoots catalogs. Williams sets it all up. Somebody else presses the button. And MoMA still gave him a whole retrospective. His dad worked in special effects in Hollywood. So he grew up on film sets, watching people manufacture things you were supposed to believe. He wanted to get away from all that. Got himself a painting studio. Sat there alone. Missed the set. So, back came the photographers. The lights. The whole production. Only now he's running it. He's not really after the pretty picture. He's after the stuff that makes them. And the pictures look like ads. Almost. A mole on a model's face. Laugh lines. Little things an ad might clean up. He leaves them. Now look at the cloth here. White, with little blue squares. Coming off the rollers, folding into a stack on a cart. Spotless. You could put it straight in a catalog. Then look at the room it's coming out of. Plaster peeling off. Brick showing through. A bare tube light. Plastic draped over the machine. A catalog photographer would find a way to lose all that. Williams wants it in the picture. The place is falling apart. And that cloth has no idea. This is Łódź, in Poland. For over a century, this was the country's cloth town. And Williams calls the whole series For Example. The rest of the title comes from a French book about two ways of running an industrial country. Capitalism, or Soviet planning. This town's tried both. Private owners ran the mills. After the war, the state took over. Then the Soviet bloc came apart. And the cloth business went with it. But look at that stack. On October first, two thousand four, somebody here was still making it.
