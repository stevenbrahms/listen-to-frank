Patchwork Quilt

Romare Bearden, 1970 · Gallery 416 · Floor 4

In art school, they called him "Petey." After Pieter Bruegel. The old Flemish painter. Peasants eating. Working. Having a party. Bearden loved those pictures. Said he wanted to paint his people the way Bruegel painted his. He's in his fifties when he finds his way into it. With scissors. At first, the collages are small. Mostly black and white. Bits of photographs, cut up and put back together. Then you get to this. Four feet wide. And now he's cutting cloth. Look at her. Pink wrap around her head. One arm reaching out, fingers spread. Legs apart. Almost like she's taking a step. Except she's lying down. He's been looking at Egyptian tomb carvings. You can see it in that flat, sideways pose. But what she's lying on looks like somebody's old bedsheet. And that quilt above her? Actual fabric. Pasted down. Southern quilts were all around him when he was a little boy. Now he's making one. Out of scraps. Find the pink stripe at her waist. Follow it up into the quilt. Same stripe. Then look at her arm. Brown forearm. Gray upper arm. She's pieced together too. MoMA buys the picture right away. The next year, Bearden gets his own show here. Only the second Black artist to have one. And this woman makes the cover. Years later, somebody asks what he was after. He brings up Rousseau's Sleeping Gypsy. The woman asleep, with a lion looking at her. Bearden puts his woman on a Southern quilt. No lion. Plenty of polka dots. He says it's a lady lying down. But he wanted something beyond a nude. Something else. "But I don't know what."
